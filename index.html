DSC 106 - Project 2 Are Countries Getting Worse on Emissions as They Urbanize?

Ethan Lam Mcallister Blair Silas Jude Rami Abukhater

Proposition: Countries are getting worse on emissions as they urbanize.

Both visualizations use the IMF/World Bank Climate Change Indicators dataset and measure emissions the same way, as CO₂ per person, so the two sides can be compared directly. Each design decision is scored from −2 (deceptive) to +2 (earnest).

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • +1.5Connected scatter with urbanization and emissions. Each country's plot shows emissions directly to urban share, so the reader can easily understand that a rightward movement means "urban growth" and an upward movement means "emissions per person increases". The axes are labeled with units that are digestible (percentage and tonnes per person) and the y-axis starts at zero.
  • −1.5Cherry-picked countries. We specifically show these six Asian urbanizers whose emissions grew, but 42 of the 134 countries that urbanized in the same time period saw CO₂ emissions per person fall. By showing all countries, a different general trend would have been exposed.
  • −1Percent-change labels. Using percentages make small starting values look alarming even if the value is small. For example, Vietnam's +897% is only 0.27 → 2.7 t, while South Korea added 6.4 t but shows +110%. Labeling by tonnes added would be more honest.
  • −1Creating generalizations with the title. The title uses the six countries to represent a continent, leaving out countries like Japan, which went from 77% to 92% urban with flat emissions. The wording is strong which may a little biased but it aligns with the high percentages and stays factual.
  • +0.5Data starts from 1990. 1990 is the standard climate-policy baseline, so it is defensible. It also measures these economies around the time of their fastest industrial growth, which is when urbanization and emissions are most likely linked. If it were a different time period (e.g., 2000-2018), the story would be most likely be different.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • +1.5Metrics stay the same. Both charts use CO₂ per person, so measurements are transparent.
  • −1Indexing both series to 1990 = 100. Indexing puts different units on one scale. These countries chosen were already mostly urban, so their urban line barely moves. Plotting raw urban share would show how little room these countries have left to urbanize.
  • −1Country selection. All six countries chosen are wealthy and already-urban Western countries, making their urbanization during this period seem small. South Korea fits in this group in terms of level of urban share, but its emissions doubled, so it is left out.
  • −0.5Shaded gap. The green fill makes the gap between the lines feel larger than just having the labels.
  • +1Small multiples with direct labels. Shared axes avoid overlapping lines, and the labels let the viewer check/compare the claim directly. The dashed 100 line along with a single legend keep the panels consistent.

Final Reflection

What surprised us was how little outright deception the final charts needed. We didn't have to manipulate. Most of the persuasive work comes from cherrypicking countries to include and metrics for change. The "for" chart reports percentages that inflate small starting values. The "against" chart includes countries that had almost no room left to urbanize. The same dataset can be adapted for both perspectives because the underlying relationship is mixed. Urbanization tracks with higher emissions across countries, largely through income, but the two are not in a one-to-one causal relationship.

To us, an ethical visualization is one where a reader who knows what was left out would still agree with the main takeaway. Persuasion is acceptable when it decides emphasis, e.g. persuasive titles or evocative coloring. It becomes misleading when the design depends on information being withheld from the viewers or intentionally displayed in a non-standard way, such as in this example when we cherrypicked the countries which best fit a given story, or using a percentage to hide a tiny absolute change. Our rough test is whether the chart would survive adding back the data we removed. Neither of ours fully would, which is why both selection decisions scored −1.5.
